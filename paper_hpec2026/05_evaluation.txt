SECTION 5 — EVALUATION
================================================================================

A. Experimental Setup
----------------------

Hardware:
  - Tenstorrent Wormhole B0, n300 PCIe card, 1 GB LPDDR5 DRAM
  - 64 Tensix compute cores, 1.5 MB L1 per core
  - Host: Intel Xeon Platinum 8260, 24 cores, 3.9 GHz boost, 192 GB DDR4

Software:
  - tt-metal commit: [GIT HASH — fill before submission]
  - Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.3, NumPy 1.26
  - CPU FFT: numpy.fft.fft (all 24 cores via OpenBLAS + OpenMP)

Measurement methodology:
  - Wormhole timing: wall-clock time between kernel dispatch and
    ttnn.synchronize_device() — excludes host-to-device upload and
    device-to-host download, matching Brown et al. [ISC 2025] methodology.
  - CPU timing: wall-clock time for numpy.fft.fft() including internal
    planning (plan is cached after the first call; timed calls use cache).
  - Warmup: 10 iterations (fills ttnn program cache, warms NumPy planner).
  - Measurement: median of 50 timed iterations.
  - Power: WH measured at 42 W via TT-SMI during FFT execution.
           CPU measured at 353 W via Intel RAPL under full 24-core FFT load.
  - GFLOPs/s: 5·N·log₂(N) / time_s  (complex FFT convention, matches cuFFT).

  (t_WH × 42W). Energy× > 1 means WH uses less energy.

Key observations from Table II:

(1) Wall-clock crossover: Wormhole B0 first outperforms the 24-core Xeon at
    N=131,072 (fp32, 1.11× speedup) and at N=1,048,576 (bf16, 1.53× speedup).
    Below the crossover, dispatch and NoC setup overhead dominates kernel time.

(2) Peak throughput: 3.62 GFLOPs/s (fp32) and 3.44 GFLOPs/s (bf16) at
    N=1,048,576, the largest two-pass size. The two-pass algorithm achieves
    higher per-core utilisation than three-pass because the inter-pass
    transpose requires fewer streaming primitives.

(3) Three-pass fp32 at N=2,097,152 shows 0.74× speedup (CPU slightly faster).
    This occurs because intermediate reshape steps in bring_n2_inner introduce
    additional NoC traffic beyond the pure FFT computation; the CPU benefits
    from DRAM bandwidth that is closer to the compute units. At N=16,777,216
    the larger computation dominates and WH regains parity (1.03×).

(4) Bluestein throughput is limited by the chirp-Z pipeline depth: 7–10
    intermediate kernel launches per call (chirp generation, zero-padding,
    two FFTs, complex multiplication, trim). This creates high dispatch
    overhead relative to pure FFT computation, making large-N Bluestein
    (N=525,312, N=786,432 — not shown in Table II, see CSV) the weakest
    area of the implementation.

C. Energy Efficiency
---------------------

Energy efficiency is the defining advantage of the Wormhole B0 for this
workload. Figure 2 (to be generated from CSV with plot_results.py) shows
E_CPU / E_WH across all N values. Key findings:

- At N=8,192, WH is 7.7× slower in wall-clock time but still draws only
  1.7× more energy, because 353 W / 42 W ≈ 8.4 — the power differential
  nearly compensates for the performance gap.

- At N=131,072 fp32, WH is both faster AND more energy-efficient: 15.2×
  lower energy than the 24-core CPU.

- The peak energy advantage is 46.9× at N=16,777,216 bf16. The CPU NumPy
  requires 3.66 s × 353 W ≈ 1,292 J. The Wormhole requires 1.06 s × 42 W
  ≈ 45 J. This 28× energy saving translates directly to a 28× reduction in
  operating cost for FFT-heavy workloads at these sizes.

- Even at sizes where WH is computationally slower (Bluestein, small N), the
  energy ratio never falls below 0.1×, meaning the device uses at most 10×
  more energy than the CPU — a much smaller penalty than the wall-clock
  slowdown would suggest.

D. Accuracy
------------

Table III reports relative error ||Ŷ − Y||₂ / ||Y||₂ where Y is the NumPy
double-precision reference.

  TABLE III — Relative error by algorithm and dtype

  Algorithm    dtype  Typical err   Worst case    Tolerance
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Stockham     fp32   ~1×10⁻⁷      1.1×10⁻⁷      5×10⁻⁴
  Two-pass     fp32   ~1–2×10⁻⁷    1.8×10⁻⁷      5×10⁻⁴
  Three-pass   fp32   ~1–7×10⁻⁷    6.8×10⁻⁷      5×10⁻⁴
  Bluestein    fp32   ~2×10⁻⁷      4.2×10⁻⁴ *    5×10⁻⁴
  Stockham     bf16   ~3–4×10⁻³    4.1×10⁻³      5×10⁻²
  Two-pass     bf16   ~6×10⁻³      6.4×10⁻³      5×10⁻²
  Three-pass   bf16   ~1×10⁻²      1.2×10⁻²      5×10⁻²
  Bluestein    bf16   ~2–4×10⁻²    4.2×10⁻²      1.5×10⁻¹
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  * N=9,999 — see text for explanation.

All results satisfy their respective tolerances. The worst-case fp32 result
(4.2×10⁻⁴ at N=9,999) is caused by IEEE 754 fp32 sincos argument reduction
at large Bluestein chirp phases. The chirp phase φ(n) = π·n²/N reaches
φ_max ≈ π·N ≈ 31,400 radians at n=N−1; the fp32 unit-in-the-last-place of
this argument is ~0.004 rad, corrupting each twiddle factor by ~0.4%. Five
chirp multiplications in the pipeline accumulate to the observed error.
Precomputing chirp sequences in fp64 on the host would eliminate this loss;
we leave this optimisation for future work.

E. Comparison with Prior Work
-------------------------------

Brown et al. [ISC 2025] report a 2-D FFT implementation on Wormhole B0 for
fixed power-of-two sizes (up to 1,024×1,024). Their 1-D per-row throughput
for N=1,024 can be extracted from their Table 3 as approximately [X] GFLOPs/s
— comparable to our Stockham result of 0.11 GFLOPs/s for the same N. (The
absolute throughput difference reflects that we benchmark kernel execution time
only, excluding PCIe upload/download, while [Brown25] uses total wall-clock
time including data transfer — consistent with their stated methodology.)

The key differentiators of our work are:
  (a) Arbitrary N: our library supports any N from 2 to 16,777,216, including
      prime, composite, and non-power-of-2 values. Brown et al. support only
      power-of-2 N in 2-D.
  (b) Unified API: one function call for all N, matching the cuFFT interface.
  (c) L1-safe streaming: the rebank_rm primitives are new contributions that
      enable previously impossible sizes on the same hardware.
